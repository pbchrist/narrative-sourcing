Does the rule actually do anything?

This tool deletes any claim whose quote does not appear in the source word for word. That is the whole design. So it is worth asking whether the rule changes what the model does, or just makes the README sound rigorous.

I measured it. The result was smaller than I expected, is not statistically significant, and turned up a failure mode I had not predicted. All three of those sentences are in this page on purpose.

Method

Twelve profiles — eight short, four long — through the same model at the same temperature, twice. The two runs differ in exactly one thing: whether the system prompt demands the evidence be copied verbatim.

The ablation removes only the verbatim requirement. Both conditions still ask for evidence. Removing evidence entirely would have proved something nobody doubted.

removed in the ablated condition Every departure and pursuit must quote VERBATIM text from the profile as its evidence. Copy the characters exactly… A quote that does not appear in the profile word for word will be discarded automatically. replaced with Every departure and pursuit should include supporting evidence from the profile.

Grading is done by src/story/verify.py — the same deterministic check the product ships. No model judges this, including me.

the honest caveat

Fisher exact, two-tailed: p = 0.057. At 83 claims this difference is still not statistically significant at the conventional threshold, though it is a great deal closer than it was. The rule may help. This experiment does not prove it does, and I would rather print the number than a verdict.

I was originally going to publish a much larger number, carried over from an earlier run. It did not reproduce when I measured it again. That is why the raw model output for all 24 runs is committed next to the code — so the numbers can be checked rather than believed.

what changed since the first measurement

These figures were re-measured after a round of fixes, and they are not a clean comparison with the ones this page carried before (0 of 37 against 3 of 40, p = 0.241). The system prompt changed in between: it now asks for a full account of a career rather than whatever the model volunteered, so both conditions produce more claims than they used to. More claims in the ablated condition means more opportunities to invent one. The direction did not change and the rule-abiding condition is still at zero, but a rate measured under one prompt and a rate measured under another are two different measurements, and I am not going to present them as one.

The interesting part is not the rate

All three failures are the same move, and it is not invention. The model splices non-contiguous fragments into something that reads as one continuous quotation:

claim: “Moving away from designing new machines toward preventing failures.”

2013-2018 Mechanical Designer, Kubota; 2018-now Reliability Engineer, Daikin

Two separate lines, joined by a semicolon that is not in the profile.

claim: “A deep, perhaps obsessive engagement with the root causes of mechanical breakdown.”

I look at why machines fail. … I read failure reports for fun, which my wife finds concerning.

Two sentences bridged by an ellipsis, with a paragraph removed between them.

Every fragment is real. The quotation is not. That is more dangerous than an invented fact, because it manufactures adjacency between things that were never adjacent — and a reader checking one fragment will find it and stop checking.

What this actually changed my mind about

  • The prompt wording is not the load-bearing part. I assumed it was.
  • The verifier is. It caught all three regardless of the prompt, because it compares characters rather than trusting instructions.
  • The failure to design against is quote-splicing, not fabrication — which is why the check requires one contiguous span and rejects a stitched one.

A 7.5% splice rate on a small sample is not a headline. But three invented quotations attributed to real people, in 40 claims, is the exact thing a recruiter would paste into an email and never think about again.
